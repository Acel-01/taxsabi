# Pensions and Voluntary Contributions: The Complete Guide

## The Contributory Pension Scheme

Nigeria's Contributory Pension Scheme is built on joint contributions: the employer and the employee each contribute a percentage of the employee's monthly emoluments into the employee's Retirement Savings Account. Under the Pension Reform Act 2014, the minimum rates are ten per cent by the employer and eight per cent by the employee. The rates can be revised upwards by agreement between the employer and the employee, and the Commission must be notified of any such revision.

The Act also allows alternatives, within limits. An employer may agree to pay additional retirement benefits, or elect to bear the full responsibility of the Scheme, in which case the employer's contribution must not be less than twenty per cent of the employee's monthly emoluments. Separately, every employer must maintain a Group Life Insurance Policy in favour of each employee for a minimum of three times the annual total emolument of the employee, with the premium paid not later than the date the cover commences.

Coverage under the Scheme extends to employees in the public service of the Federation, the Federal Capital Territory, the states, local governments, and the private sector, subject to the exemptions in the Act. Even categories outside the mandatory net — including persons covered under the special provisions of the Act and exempted persons — are entitled to make voluntary contributions under the Scheme.

## The Retirement Savings Account

Every employee covered by the Scheme maintains a Retirement Savings Account, referred to as an RSA, in their own name, with a Pension Fund Administrator of their choice. The employee notifies the employer of the chosen Pension Fund Administrator and the identity of the account. From then on, the employer's duties are mechanical and time-bound:

- Deduct the employee's monthly contribution at source.
- Remit both the employee's contribution and the employer's contribution to the Pension Fund Custodian specified by the employee's Pension Fund Administrator, not later than **seven working days** from the day the employee is paid.

The seven-working-day clock is one of the most commonly missed deadlines in payroll. It is not the same clock as PAYE, which follows the 10th-of-the-following-month rhythm. And the penalty for missing it is significant: an employer who fails to deduct or remit within the time is liable to a penalty stipulated by the Commission, with a statutory floor of not less than **2 percent of the total unpaid contribution for each month or part of a month** that the default continues. The penalty is recoverable as a debt owed to the employee's retirement savings account — the money effectively goes to the employee's account, not to the authority alone.

## Tax treatment of pension contributions and benefits

The tax treatment is generous by design, because the policy intent is to encourage retirement saving:

- **Contributions are deductible.** Contributions to the Scheme form part of tax-deductible expenses in computing tax payable by an employer or employee under the relevant income tax law, and the Taxation Act lists the individual's pension contributions among eligible deductions.
- **Pension fund income is not taxable.** All interests, dividends, profits, investment and other income accruable to pension funds and assets are not taxable.
- **Retirement benefits are not taxable.** Any amount payable as a retirement benefit is not taxable, and the Taxation Act exempts pension funds and assets created under the Pension Reform Act, as well as pension, gratuity, or retirement benefits granted in accordance with it.

In the simplest terms: contribute before tax, grow without tax, and draw benefits without tax — provided the rules are respected.

## Voluntary contributions: the flexible top-up

A voluntary contribution is an additional contribution an employee makes to their own RSA, over and above the mandatory contributions being made by the employee and the employer. The purpose is to enhance future retirement benefits. It is also the one pension lever an employee can adjust to suit their own plans — which makes it central to any tax-efficiency strategy for employees.

### Who can make them

Eligible contributors include employees in organisations with three or more staff who are already making mandatory contributions under the Scheme; workers and retirees in organisations operating a Closed Pension Fund Administration or with an Approved Existing Scheme; retirees receiving pension under the Scheme who take up contract employment; retirees under the defunct Defined Benefit Scheme who take up contract employment; and categories of constitutional office holders and other persons identified in the guidelines. Persons exempted from mandatory coverage are similarly entitled to make voluntary contributions under the Scheme.

### The rules of the road

- **Currency and account.** Voluntary contributions are made only in Nigerian currency, into and out of a duly registered Retirement Savings Account managed by a licensed Pension Fund Administrator.
- **Through the employer.** The employee notifies the employer in writing of the intention to make voluntary contributions and the amount to be deducted from emoluments; all voluntary contributions are remitted through the employer into the RSA. There is no freestyle channel.
- **Size limit.** The contribution must be made from the employee's legitimate income and must not exceed one-third of the month's salary, in line with the Labour Act.
- **Frequency.** No more than once a month for any category of contributor.
- **Structure.** For active contributors, the voluntary contribution section of the RSA is split in two: 50% is contingent — available for withdrawal under the guidelines — and 50% is fixed for pension and only utilised at retirement to augment pension.
- **Access.** Voluntary contributions made by active contributors must be retained in the RSA for a minimum of two years before access, and after the first withdrawal the contributor may withdraw once every two years, with subsequent withdrawals based on incremental contributions. The two-year rule does not apply to certain other categories, such as contract-employment retirees, whose contributions are accessed at the expiration or termination of the contract.

### The tax rule that keeps it honest

Voluntary contributions receive the same deductibility as other contributions to the Scheme. But any income earned on a voluntary contribution is subject to tax at the point of withdrawal if the withdrawal is made before the end of five years from the date the contribution was made. In other words: contribute, deduct, and leave it to grow — the income stays outside the tax net over the long horizon. Withdraw early and the income earned on the way is taxed.

### Starting a voluntary contribution: the practical steps

1. Confirm eligibility and that the RSA is active.
2. Decide the monthly amount, respecting the one-third-of-salary cap and the employee's cash needs.
3. Notify the employer in writing of the intention and the amount.
4. The employer deducts and remits to the Pension Fund Custodian specified by the Pension Fund Administrator, alongside the mandatory contributions.
5. Keep the records: RSA statements segregate mandatory from voluntary contributions, and statements are the evidence trail for both contributions and any eventual withdrawal.

When the time comes to withdraw, the Pension Fund Administrator collects a Voluntary Contributions Withdrawal Form and specified documents — for an active contributor, a letter of employment to evidence continued service; for other categories, the documents that establish the relevant status. Withdrawals are processed through the administrator and reported to the Commission.

## What voluntary contributions are worth: the arithmetic

The tax value of a voluntary contribution is its amount multiplied by the marginal rate of the band it removes income from. Three illustrations:

- An employee with chargeable income of N3,000,000 sits in the 15% band. A voluntary contribution of N150,000 reduces chargeable income to N2,850,000 and the tax from N330,000 to N307,500 — a saving of N22,500, or 15% of the contribution.
- An employee with chargeable income of N13,000,000 sits in the 21% band. A monthly voluntary contribution of N25,000 (N300,000 over a year, comfortably within the one-third cap for a salary of N100,000 or more per month) reduces tax by N63,000 — 21% of the contribution.
- An employee with chargeable income above N50,000,000 sits in the 25% band. A contribution of N1,000,000 saves N250,000 of tax. For this employee, the effective cost of N1,000,000 of retirement savings is N750,000 — and the contribution sits in an account where the investment income, if left for five years, is not taxed on withdrawal, and the eventual retirement benefit is not taxed at all.

The same logic runs in reverse, which is why discipline matters: an employee who over-commits to voluntary contributions and then withdraws within five years can trigger tax on the income earned, and the contingent/fixed split means only half of the voluntary balance is even available for withdrawal. Voluntary contributions are powerful precisely because they are locked-in retirement money; that lock is a feature, not a bug, and the amount should be set with both the tax rate and the cash-flow plan in view.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking any amount can be contributed.** The cap is one-third of the month's salary, and only legitimate income qualifies.
- **Bypassing the employer.** All voluntary contributions flow through the employer into the RSA.
- **Confusing the clocks.** Mandatory pension contributions are due within seven working days of pay day; PAYE is due by the 10th of the following month.
- **Withdrawing too early.** Income earned on contributions withdrawn within five years is taxed; the contingent portion also requires the two-year retention and the once-every-two-years rhythm.
- **Keeping no evidence.** Contributions are deductible and required evidence in filing practice. RSA statements and remittance records are the proof.
