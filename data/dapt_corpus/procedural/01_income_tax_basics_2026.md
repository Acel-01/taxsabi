# Income Tax Basics for the 2026 Year of Assessment

## The framework that applies from 1 January 2026

From 1 January 2026, individual income tax in Nigeria is governed by the Nigeria Tax Act 2025. The Act applies throughout Nigeria to any person required to comply with any of its provisions, whether personally or on behalf of another person. Worked examples, deduction claims, and payroll decisions for the 2026 year of assessment all follow this Act and the Nigeria Tax Administration Act 2025.

Two consequences of the new framework are worth stating plainly at the outset:

- The consolidated relief allowance that older Nigerian tax guides describe no longer exists. The Act contains no "personal relief" or "consolidated relief allowance" concept at all. Relief from income tax for individuals is now delivered through two mechanisms: the zero-rate band in the tax table and the specific eligible deductions listed in section 30.
- Individuals who earn the national minimum wage are exempt from income tax on their chargeable income. The minimum wage is set by the National Minimum Wage Act, as amended in 2024, at not less than N70,000 per month.

## How the tax is computed

The computation follows a simple chain:

1. **Total income.** Start with the individual's total income for the year from all sources, including employment income.
2. **Eligible deductions.** Subtract the deductions the individual is entitled to claim under the Act — contributions to the National Housing Fund, the National Health Insurance Scheme, and the Pension Reform Act; interest on a loan for developing an owner-occupied home; qualifying life insurance and annuity premiums; and rent relief. Deductions are not automatic: the Act requires them to be claimed in writing in the prescribed form, and the tax authority may require documentary evidence before allowing them.
3. **Chargeable income.** The result is the chargeable income — total income less eligible deductions.
4. **Apply the tax table.** Tax is calculated by applying the graduated bands in the Fourth Schedule to the chargeable income.

## The 2026 tax bands

| Band | Amount | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| First | N800,000 | 0% |
| Next | N2,200,000 | 15% |
| Next | N9,000,000 | 18% |
| Next | N13,000,000 | 21% |
| Next | N25,000,000 | 23% |
| Above | N50,000,000 | 25% |

The cumulative boundaries of the bands are N800,000; N3,000,000; N12,000,000; N25,000,000; and N50,000,000. A useful way to read the table is by cumulative income: income up to N800,000 is taxed at 0%; income between N800,000 and N3,000,000 is taxed at 15%; income between N3,000,000 and N12,000,000 at 18%; income between N12,000,000 and N25,000,000 at 21%; income between N25,000,000 and N50,000,000 at 23%; and every naira above N50,000,000 at 25%.

Because the bands are graduated, the rate that applies to the last naira of chargeable income — the marginal rate — is higher than the average rate. An individual with N3,162,000 of chargeable income is in the 18% band, but their average rate is far lower, because the first N800,000 is untaxed and the next N2,200,000 attracts only 15%.

## A worked example

Take an employee with gross income of N3,600,000 for the 2026 year and eligible deductions of N438,000 recorded and supported by documentation.

- Chargeable income: N3,600,000 - N438,000 = N3,162,000.
- Tax on the first N800,000 at 0%: N0.
- Tax on the next N2,200,000 at 15%: N330,000.
- Tax on the remaining N162,000 (the part of chargeable income between N3,000,000 and N3,162,000) at 18%: N29,160.
- Total tax: N330,000 + N29,160 = N359,160.

Notice how the arithmetic works: chargeable income is sliced through the bands in order, and each slice is taxed at its own rate. The highest rate reached in this example is 18%, and only N162,000 of income actually sits in that band.

## The minimum wage exemption

The tax table in the Fourth Schedule applies to every individual other than "an individual earning the Minimum Wage in line with the Minimum Wage Act". An individual earning the national minimum wage — not less than N70,000 per month, under the 2024 amendment to the National Minimum Wage Act — is therefore outside the chargeable-income tax computation altogether. This is a status tied to earning the minimum wage as defined by that Act; it is not a general exemption for low earners at other income levels.

## Employment income and PAYE

Most individuals who work in the formal sector pay through the Pay-As-You-Earn system. Their employer deducts income tax from their emoluments as they are paid, and remits it to the relevant state tax authority. Because the employer deducts at source, the tax is collected during the year rather than at the end. The employer must ensure that the aggregate of the deductions over the year equals the employee's income tax for that year. Employees are not passive in the system: the annual return framework expects employees to file a return of their income from all sources, including employment income.

The practical lesson for individuals is that the tax deducted from a payslip is a running instalment of the year's liability, not a final settlement. Reliefs that the employee is entitled to but has not claimed — rent relief, pension-related deductions, qualifying insurance premiums — reduce the year's true liability, and the sooner they are claimed in writing with evidence, the sooner the payroll position can be reconciled with the correct final figure.

## What changed, and what people still get wrong

Many older guides, templates, and payroll sheets in circulation were built around the repealed Personal Income Tax Act framework. Two errors follow from that:

- Applying a consolidated relief allowance to reduce chargeable income. There is no such allowance under the current Act. Rent relief, capped at 20% of annual rent paid up to a maximum of N500,000, is a different and narrower relief.
- Assuming the old rate structure still applies. The 2026 bands are the ones in the Fourth Schedule of the Nigeria Tax Act 2025, with a top rate of 25% and six graduated steps.

Getting the computation right starts with getting the framework right: the correct statute, the correct bands, the correct deduction list, and the claim and evidence discipline that the Act requires.

## Records and evidence

Eligible deductions are not granted on the strength of an oral claim. The Act requires a claim in writing, in the form the relevant tax authority prescribes. The authority may then call for documentary evidence in support of the claim, and where that evidence is absent or inadequate, it may refuse the deduction or allow only part of the amount claimed. That single sentence of the statute — evidence in, deduction granted; evidence missing, deduction refused — is the foundation of every tax-planning conversation. The best time to assemble evidence is while the year is running, not after it has closed.
