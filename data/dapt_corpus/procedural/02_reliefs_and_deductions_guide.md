# Reliefs and Deductions: A Practical Guide

## What an eligible deduction is

Under section 30 of the Nigeria Tax Act 2025, chargeable income is total income less eligible deductions. The eligible deductions are set out in a single list in section 30(2)(a). They are:

1. The individual's contributions under the National Housing Fund.
2. The individual's contributions under the National Health Insurance Scheme.
3. The individual's contributions under the Pension Reform Act.
4. Interest on loans for developing an owner-occupied residential house.
5. The annual amount of any annuity or premium paid by the individual during the year preceding the year of assessment in respect of insurance on his life or the life of his spouse, or a contract for a deferred annuity on his own life or the life of his spouse.
6. Rent relief: 20% of annual rent paid, subject to a maximum of N500,000, whichever is lower — provided the individual accurately declares the actual amount of rent paid and other relevant information prescribed by the relevant tax authority.

There is no consolidated relief allowance in the current framework. The list above, together with the zero-rate band, is the whole of the individual relief system.

## How a deduction becomes real: claiming and evidence

Two statutory rules govern every claim:

- **The claim must be in writing**, in the form the relevant tax authority prescribes. A deduction "shall not be allowed under this Part to any person for a year of assessment, unless claimed in writing in such form as the relevant tax authority may prescribe."
- **Evidence may be demanded.** The relevant tax authority may require a claimant to produce documentary evidence in support of the claim, and where the evidence is absent or inadequate, it may refuse the deduction or such part of the amount claimed.

Filing practice reinforces the point. When returns are filed, evidence of the underlying contributions and payments is required for pension, life assurance premium, NHIS, and NHF claims — these are mandatory for the relevant reliefs to be granted. Supporting documents expected with a return include the tax form, financial statements where applicable, the tax computation, and evidence of taxes already paid at source.

A deduction the individual never claims, or cannot substantiate, simply does not exist for tax purposes. This is why record-keeping during the year matters more than arithmetic at the end of it.

## Filing mechanics: where and when claims happen

**Where.** The annual income tax return is the vehicle for relief claims. In Lagos State, returns are filed on the LIRS e-Tax platform (`www.etax.lirs.net`) using the duly completed Income Tax Form — **Tax Form A** — which is described as the return of income *and claims for reliefs and allowances*. Supporting documents are uploaded with the return: the tax computation, and evidence of contributions and payments such as pension, life assurance premiums, NHIS, and NHF records. Claims on paper that never make it into the return, or into the prescribed form, do not count.

**Who must file.** Every taxable person files an annual return for each year of assessment, without notice or demand. That includes employees whose tax is fully deducted under PAYE: filing is an individual obligation, not something the employer discharges on the employee's behalf. An employee with only employment income still files, and still claims reliefs, in that return.

**When and how the relief takes effect.** This is the part that surprises people, so it is worth stating carefully. The income tax is an annual liability; PAYE is simply the monthly instalment method of collecting it. Reliefs are claimed in the annual return and reduce the **year's** assessed liability. Under the self-assessment framework, the return the taxpayer files is treated as an assessment of the amount payable — including a nil or negative amount — and the tax authority may accept it, adjust it, or reject it and determine the tax itself.

The practical consequence for timing is this: reliefs do not switch on "from the month of approval" or operate as a retroactive re-run of past monthly payroll. The relief's benefit attaches to the **year of assessment** to which it relates. If PAYE deducted during the year exceeds the liability as corrected by the reliefs, the difference is settled through the refund and credit machinery:

- overpaid tax is refunded after an audit by the relevant tax authority, with the option of a set-off against any tax liability instead;
- refunds are to be made within 90 days of the authority's decision;
- the claim for refund must be made in writing within six years after the end of the year of assessment to which it relates;
- in practice, an excess credit (for example, withholding tax or PAYE credits exceeding the tax due) may be applied against future tax liabilities or refunded, subject to supporting documentation.

So if a claim with the required evidence is filed and assessed for a given year, the relief reduces that year's liability and any over-deduction comes back as a refund or credit. If an assessment does not reflect a relief that was properly claimed, the remedy is a written notice of objection within **30 days** of service of the notice of assessment, setting out the specific issues and the amendment required.

**The same process for every relief.** Rent relief, pension contributions, NHF and NHIS contributions, mortgage interest, and life insurance or annuity premiums all ride the same machinery: claimed in the annual return, supported by evidence, assessed for the year, and reconciled through payment, credit, or refund. The differences between them are about *what evidence* proves the claim and *when the underlying payment happens* — not about separate procedures.

**One warning.** False claims are expensive. A refund obtained through a false or fictitious claim attracts recovery of the amount, an administrative penalty of 50% of the amount wrongly received, and interest at the prevailing Central Bank of Nigeria Monetary Policy Rate. Claims should be accurate, documented, and timely.

## The deductions, one by one

### Contributions under the National Housing Fund

An individual's contributions under the National Housing Fund are deductible. The obligation to contribute depends on the sector: under the NHF Act as amended, private-sector participation is voluntary — employees "may contribute" — while the public sector is covered by a mandatory arrangement. Either way, where the individual contributes, the contribution is an eligible deduction and should be documented so it can be claimed and supported.

### Contributions under the National Health Insurance Scheme

The individual's contributions under the National Health Insurance Scheme are deductible. As with NHF contributions, the deduction follows the contribution: it is the individual's own contribution that is claimed, and evidence of the contribution is required in filing practice.

### Contributions under the Pension Reform Act

The individual's pension contributions are deductible. For employees in the Contributory Pension Scheme, contributions are made as a percentage of monthly emoluments and remitted by the employer to the employee's Retirement Savings Account. The mandatory rates, the voluntary contribution mechanism, and the tax treatment of withdrawals are covered in detail in the pension guide. What matters here is that the deduction category is broad: it covers the individual's contributions under the Pension Reform Act, which is why both mandatory deductions from salary and qualifying voluntary contributions can reduce chargeable income. Evidence of pension contributions is one of the categories required in filing practice.

### Interest on loans for developing an owner-occupied residential house

Interest on a loan taken to develop an owner-occupied residential house is deductible. "Owner-occupied" matters: the relief is tied to the home the individual lives in, not to investment property. The amount claimed is the interest on the loan, and as with every other deduction, the claim should be made in writing and supported by documentary evidence — the loan agreement and evidence of interest paid are the natural records to keep.

### Life insurance and annuity premiums

The annual amount of any annuity or premium paid by the individual during the year preceding the year of assessment, for insurance on the individual's life or the life of a spouse — or a contract for a deferred annuity on the individual's own life or the life of a spouse — is deductible. The statutory language carries a timing detail: the premium or annuity amount is measured in the year preceding the year of assessment. Evidence of life assurance premiums is required in filing practice, so policy documents and payment receipts should be retained.

### Rent relief

Rent relief is 20% of the annual rent paid, subject to a maximum of N500,000, whichever is lower. Two conditions sit alongside the arithmetic:

- The individual must accurately declare the actual amount of rent paid, together with other relevant information the tax authority prescribes. Inflating a rent figure does not create a larger deduction; it creates a false declaration.
- Evidence matters. Rent relief is claimed, not assumed, and the claim must be capable of being supported.

A rent of N2,400,000 a year produces a relief of N480,000 — 20% of the rent, below the N500,000 cap. A rent of N3,000,000 produces N600,000 by percentage, but the relief is capped at N500,000. And a rent of N1,500,000 produces N300,000. In each case the relief is the lower of the two figures.

## Common mistakes that cost money

- **Not claiming at all.** Deductions are claimed in writing in the prescribed form. An eligible deduction that is never claimed does nothing.
- **Claiming without evidence.** The authority may refuse a deduction, or part of it, where evidence is absent or inadequate. Claims made early with records are safer than large claims made late without them.
- **Confusing rent relief with the old allowance.** Rent relief is capped at N500,000 and based on actual rent paid. It is not a general allowance against income.
- **Assuming the employer handles everything.** The employer runs PAYE on the salary it pays, but the individual's reliefs — especially rent relief and qualifying insurance — depend on the individual's own claims and documentation.
- **Keeping no pension records.** Pension contributions are deductible and are among the items for which filing practice requires evidence. Statements from the pension administrator should be kept, not just payslip deductions.

## Putting the guide to work

The practical sequence for an employee is straightforward: identify which deduction categories apply, gather the evidence as the year runs, make sure each claim is put in writing in the prescribed form, and only then rely on the deduction in the tax computation. Planning value comes from knowing the categories early, because several of them — pension contributions in particular — can be adjusted during the year, while others, such as rent actually paid, are facts to be documented rather than decisions to be made.
