# PAYE and Payroll: What Employers and Employees Should Know

## The PAYE system in one paragraph

Pay-As-You-Earn is the collection mechanism for income tax on employment income. The employer deducts income tax from the employee's emoluments as they are paid and remits it to the relevant tax authority. The employer is not making a discretionary judgment about the employee's tax: the Act requires that income tax chargeable on an employee be deducted from any emolument payable, or from any payment made on account of the emolument, by the employer to the employee. The employer must also ensure that the aggregate of the deductions across the year equals the employee's income tax for that year. PAYE is therefore a year-long instalment system that should settle exactly, not approximately.

## Employer obligations

- **Deduct at source.** Tax is deducted from emoluments when they are paid.
- **Remit by the deadline.** Tax deducted from salaries is due to the relevant state tax authority on or before the 10th day of the month following the month in which it was deducted. Deductions from January payroll, for example, should be remitted by 10 February. The statutory penalty framework for failures to deduct or remit is deliberately expensive: the unremitted amount remains payable, with an administrative penalty on it and interest, so late remittance is never a neutral cash-flow decision.
- **File monthly returns.** Every person who has an obligation to deduct and remit tax renders monthly returns to the appropriate tax authority.
- **File the annual employer return.** An employer must file a return with the relevant tax authority for all emoluments paid to its employees, not later than 31 January of each year, covering all employees in its employment in the preceding year. The return discloses, for each employee: gross emoluments, including allowances and benefits in kind; total deductions; net emoluments; and the tax deducted.

The monthly and annual returns are not paperwork for its own sake. They are how the tax authority reconciles what was deducted with what was due, and how an employee's year-end position is verified.

## Employee obligations

Employees have duties too. Employees file an annual return of income from all sources, including employment income. Employment income is not the whole story of a person's taxable income, and the return is the formal place where the year's income and deductions come together. Employees should also expect that the deductions and reliefs they want recognized need to be claimed in writing, in the prescribed form, with documentary evidence where required.

## Reading a payslip

A payslip under a proper PAYE system shows more than a take-home figure. The return framework describes what the employer reports per employee, and the payslip should be reflective of the same picture:

- **Gross emoluments** — including allowances and benefits in kind.
- **Total deductions** — which typically include PAYE tax, pension contributions, and scheme contributions such as NHF and NHIS where applicable.
- **Net emoluments** — what is actually paid.
- **Tax deducted** — the PAYE for that period.

The most useful way to read a payslip is to compare it to the year's cumulative picture. Because the employer's deductions must aggregate to the year's tax liability, the payslip is a running instalment schedule. If reliefs change — a new rent relief claim, a changed voluntary pension contribution — the run rate can and should change with them.

## The remittance clocks are different

Employers running payroll have several separate remittance obligations with different clocks:

- **PAYE** is due on or before the 10th day of the following month.
- **Pension contributions** — the employee's contribution deducted at source and the employer's own contribution — must be remitted to the Pension Fund Custodian not later than seven working days from the day the employee is paid. The penalty for failing to deduct or remit within that time is set by the Commission, and the statute puts the floor at not less than 2% of the total unpaid contribution for each month or part of each month the default continues, recoverable as a debt owed to the employee's retirement savings account.
- **Withholding tax** deducted from payments to suppliers, service providers and others follows its own schedule: remittance to the federal tax authority is due by the 21st of the following month, and remittance to a state internal revenue service by the 30th of the following month.

Several clocks, several sets of penalties. A payroll function that treats them as one deadline will eventually be late on one of them.

## Reconciliation, refunds, and corrections

Payroll is a running instalment schedule, and the annual return is where the year closes. Three mechanics matter to employees:

- **Everyone files.** An employee whose tax was fully deducted under PAYE still files an individual annual return. Reliefs are claimed there, not merely reflected on a payslip.
- **Over-deduction comes back.** If PAYE deducted over the year exceeds the liability after reliefs, the excess is refunded or set off against other tax liabilities. Refunds follow an audit by the tax authority, are to be made within 90 days of the authority's decision, and require a written claim within six years after the end of the year of assessment concerned.
- **Assessments can move in both directions.** The authority may raise additional assessments — within six years of an assessment — and a taxpayer who disputes a notice of assessment must object in writing within 30 days of service, setting out the specific issues, the amendment sought, and the justification. Waiting past the window gives the assessment the last word.

## Common compliance failures

- **Treating PAYE as a negotiation.** Tax deducted at source belongs to the tax authority from the moment of deduction. Holding it back creates a liability that grows with penalties and interest.
- **Ignoring the annual return.** The 31 January employer return is a statutory filing, with employee-level detail on emoluments and tax deducted.
- **Letting pension remittance slip.** Seven working days is a short window; the penalty is calculated per month or part of a month of default.
- **Running payroll without the employee's relief claims.** Reliefs claimed and documented by the employee should be reflected in the year's deduction profile. Unclaimed reliefs are not the employer's liability in the end, but reconciling them late means avoidable over-deduction during the year.
- **Confusing gross and net.** Returns and payslips distinguish gross emoluments, total deductions, and net emoluments for a reason: benefits in kind are part of gross even when they never pass through the employee's bank account.

## A reconciliation routine that works

1. Keep employee records current: appointment, salary, allowances, benefits in kind, pension details, and the employee's claim documents.
2. Run monthly payroll deductions; remit PAYE by the 10th of the following month and pension contributions within seven working days of pay day; file the monthly returns.
3. Update the cumulative position each month, watching that deductions to date track the projected annual liability.
4. Assemble the annual employer return by 31 January, with per-employee gross emoluments, deductions, net emoluments, and tax deducted.
5. Be ready for employee questions with records: payslips, remittance evidence, and the documents supporting each employee's reliefs.

Employers who run this routine find year-end is a reconciliation, not a reconstruction. That is the entire point of the system.
